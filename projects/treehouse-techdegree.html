How to Succeed in the Treehouse Front-End Techdegree

A guide adapted from an Office Hours session I led as a student mentor in Treehouse’s Front-End Techdegree.

Make use of the Slack community

When I started, I almost skipped Slack. I thought I could work through the program alone, but the community turned out to be valuable for getting help and learning alongside other students.

Tip: Introduce yourself, ask questions, and participate in conversations when you can.

Do peer reviews

Reviewing another student’s project shows you different ways to approach the same problem. It’s also a useful way to strengthen your own troubleshooting skills.

Tip: Aim to review a project or two each week once you're eligible.

Customize your projects

I initially focused on meeting each rubric requirement. Later, I went back and redesigned projects to show more of my personality and interests. Those changes also helped me practice making independent design decisions.

Tip: Try adding thoughtful features or design choices while continuing to meet the required criteria.

Push beyond the minimum

Treehouse projects can be assessed as Meets Expectations or Exceeds Expectations. Going beyond the baseline pushes you to research, experiment, and solve new problems.

Tip: Treat the extra requirements as opportunities to practice solving unfamiliar challenges.

Leave useful notes for reviewers

Use the project submission comments to explain decisions and mention features you want reviewed. It gives reviewers context and makes the feedback more useful.

Get comfortable with ambiguity

Later assignments leave more decisions to the student. That reflects how development works outside a guided course: you won't always be given a tutorial for every library or problem.

For example, one project requires working with Chart.js without a matching course walking through every step. Researching and testing approaches is part of the learning process.

The takeaway

The goal isn't only to complete the certificate. It's to build confidence in figuring things out, reviewing other people's work, and approaching unfamiliar technical problems.
